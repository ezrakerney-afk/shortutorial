Photography Basics

Take sharper, brighter, better-looking photos with any camera, even your phone. Learn how exposure works, play with a live exposure simulator, then learn the composition and lighting habits that make the biggest difference.

01

What exposure means

A photo is just light recorded by a sensor. Exposure is how much light the sensor collects. Too little and the photo is dark and muddy (underexposed). Too much and bright areas turn pure white with no detail (overexposed, or “blown out”).

Photographers measure light in stops. One stop more means twice as much light. One stop less means half. Every camera setting below moves exposure in stops, which is why they can trade off against each other.

  • Auto mode picks all the settings for you. It's fine most of the time.
  • Learning the settings lets you choose how the photo looks: a blurry background, a frozen splash, or a silky waterfall.
  • Your camera's light meter aims for a medium brightness. That's why snow often looks gray and a black cat looks washed out. You may need to nudge exposure up for bright scenes and down for dark ones.

Rule of thumb: it's easier to brighten a slightly dark photo later than to recover a blown-out sky. When in doubt, protect the highlights.

02

The exposure triangle

Three settings control exposure. Each one also changes something else about the picture. That side effect is how you choose between them.

Aperture (f-stop)The size of the hole in the lens. Controls light and how much is in focus front to back (depth of field).
Shutter speedHow long the sensor sees light. Controls light and motion: freeze it or blur it.
ISOHow much the signal is amplified. Brightens the photo but adds grain (noise).

Aperture

Written as f/1.8, f/2.8, f/4, f/5.6, f/8, f/11, f/16. The numbers feel backwards: a small f-number is a big opening. Each step in that list is one stop.

  • f/1.8 to f/2.8: lots of light, blurry background. Great for portraits and low light.
  • f/8 to f/11: less light, most of the scene sharp. Great for landscapes and groups.

Shutter speed

Written in seconds: 1/1000, 1/500, 1/250, 1/125, 1/60, 1/30. Each halving or doubling is one stop.

  • 1/500 or faster: freezes sports, kids and pets.
  • 1/60 to 1/125: everyday handheld shots of still people.
  • Slower than about 1/60: risk of blur from shaky hands. Brace yourself, or use a tripod.
  • Seconds long: on a tripod, turns waterfalls silky and car lights into streaks.

ISO

Usually 100 to 6400 and up. Doubling the ISO is one stop brighter. Use the lowest ISO that still lets you get a fast enough shutter speed. Raise it in dim light rather than getting a blurry photo; a little grain is better than a smeared one.

Example · trading stops
  1. Start: f/8, 1/125 s, ISO 100. Exposure looks right.
  2. Want a blurry background? Open to f/4. That's 2 stops more light.
  3. Balance it: shutter 2 stops faster, 1/125 → 1/250 → 1/500.
  4. f/4, 1/500 s, ISO 100: same brightness, softer background.

04

Composition: where things go

Composition is how you arrange things in the frame. A few simple habits make photos look intentional instead of accidental.

Rule of thirds

Imagine the frame split by two lines across and two lines down, like a tic-tac-toe board. Put your subject on one of the lines, or where two meet, instead of dead center. Put the horizon on the top or bottom line, not through the middle. Leave space in front of a person's face or a moving subject, so they have somewhere to look or go.

Try it · place the subject

Move the runner around the frame. The grid shows where the strong points are.

Leading lines

Roads, fences, rivers, railings and shorelines pull the viewer's eye through the picture. Stand so a line starts near a bottom corner and runs toward your subject. Diagonal lines feel more energetic than straight horizontal ones.

More composition habits

  • Fill the frame. Step closer. Most beginner photos have the subject too small.
  • Check the edges. Before you press the shutter, look around the border for trash cans, half-people and poles growing out of heads.
  • Simple background. Move a few steps so the background is plain: sky, a wall, distant trees.
  • Framing. Shoot through a doorway, window or branches to put a natural frame around the subject.
  • Change height. Get down to eye level with kids and pets. Shoot from low for drama, from above for food.
  • Keep it level. A tilted horizon is the quickest way to make a nice photo look off.

Rules are starting points. Centered works great for symmetry (a doorway, a reflection) and for bold, simple subjects. Break the rule on purpose, not by accident.

05

Light matters most

The same subject can look flat or stunning depending on the light. Pay attention to three things: how much light there is, its direction, and whether it's hard or soft.

LightLooks likeGood for
Golden hour (about the hour after sunrise and before sunset)Warm, low, long shadowsPortraits, landscapes, almost anything
Midday sunHard, harsh shadows, squintingBold colors, architecture. Move people into shade.
Overcast day or open shadeSoft, even, flatteringPortraits, flowers, food
Window lightSoft and directionalIndoor portraits, food, products
Blue hour (just after sunset)Deep blue sky, glowing city lightsCityscapes on a tripod
  • Front light (sun behind you) is safe but flat.
  • Side light shows texture and shape. Turn your subject toward a window at a slight angle.
  • Back light (sun behind the subject) gives a glowing outline but can make faces dark. Tap the face to expose for it, or use fill flash.
  • Built-in flash straight on looks harsh. Use it outdoors to fill shadows, and turn it off indoors when you can use window light instead.

Never look at or point the camera at the sun through an optical viewfinder or long lens. It can damage your eyes and the camera sensor.

06

Phone camera tips

Your phone handles exposure automatically, but you can still steer it. These work on iPhone and Android, though menu names vary.

  1. Wipe the lens. Pocket smudges cause most hazy, glowy phone photos. Use a soft cloth.
  2. Turn on the grid. iPhone: Settings > Camera > Grid. On Android, look for “Grid lines” in the camera app's settings. Use it for the rule of thirds and level horizons.
  3. Tap to focus, slide to brighten. Tap your subject to set focus and exposure, then drag the brightness control (the sun icon on iPhone, a slider on most Android phones) up or down. Press and hold to lock it.
  4. Use the lenses, not pinch zoom. Tap 0.5×, 1×, 2× or higher if your phone has those lenses. Pinching between them crops the image and loses detail. Better yet, walk closer.
  5. Hold still. Tuck your elbows in, breathe out, and tap gently. In low light, Night mode keeps the shutter open for a few seconds, so hold steady until it finishes or lean on something.
  6. Try Portrait mode for people and pets. It fakes the blurry background of a wide aperture.
  7. Take a burst for action (hold the shutter or swipe it, depending on your phone) and keep the sharpest one.
  8. Edit lightly. Straighten, crop, and adjust brightness first. Heavy filters date quickly.

A 7-day practice plan

  • Day 1: 20 photos with the grid on, subject on a third every time.
  • Day 2: Find 5 leading lines in your neighborhood.
  • Day 3: Same object by a window at 3 different angles.
  • Day 4: Golden hour walk.
  • Day 5: Freeze motion: a pet, a kid, water from a faucet.
  • Day 6: Low light without flash. Brace the phone.
  • Day 7: Pick your 5 best and ask why they work.

FAQCommon questions

Do I need an expensive camera to take good photos?

No. Composition and light matter far more than gear. Modern phones are excellent in good light. A dedicated camera mostly helps with fast action, low light, and real background blur.

What mode should a beginner use on a camera?

Try aperture priority (A or Av). You choose the f-stop and the camera picks the shutter speed. Turn on auto ISO and keep an eye on the shutter speed so it doesn't get too slow.

Why are my photos blurry?

Either the subject moved (shutter too slow), the camera moved (shaky hands), or focus landed on the wrong spot. Use a faster shutter speed, brace yourself, and tap or point the focus on your subject.

Why are my photos grainy?

High ISO in dim light. Add light (move to a window, turn on lamps), use a wider aperture, or steady the camera so you can use a slower shutter and lower ISO.

Should I shoot RAW?

RAW files keep more detail for editing, especially in highlights and shadows, but they're bigger and need editing. JPEG or HEIC is fine while you're learning.
