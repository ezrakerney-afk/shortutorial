Newton's Three Laws of Motion

Why you lurch forward when a bus brakes, why it's harder to push a full trolley, and how rockets fly. Three rules from 1687 explain them all.

01

First law: things keep doing what they're doing

An object stays still, or keeps moving at the same speed in a straight line, unless a force acts on it. This tendency is called inertia.

  • When a bus brakes, your body keeps moving forward. That's why seatbelts matter.
  • A puck on ice glides a long way because there's little friction to slow it.
02

Second law: force = mass × acceleration

F = m × aforce in newtons (N) · mass in kilograms (kg) · acceleration in metres per second squared (m/s²)

The heavier something is, the more force it takes to speed it up by the same amount. Push a full shopping trolley and an empty one equally hard: the empty one speeds up much faster.

Example · a 1,000 kg car accelerating at 3 m/s²
  1. F = 1,000 × 3
  2. 3,000 N of force

03

Third law: every push has a push back

For every action there's an equal and opposite reaction. Forces always come in pairs.

  • A rocket pushes hot gas down; the gas pushes the rocket up.
  • When you walk, your foot pushes the ground backwards; the ground pushes you forwards.
  • Jump off a small boat and it drifts backwards as you go forwards.

Why doesn't everything cancel out? The two forces act on different objects. The ground isn't pushed by its own force; you are.
