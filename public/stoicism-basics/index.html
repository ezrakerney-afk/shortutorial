01

What Stoicism is (and isn't)

Stoicism is a school of philosophy founded in Athens around 300 BCE by Zeno of Citium. He taught at the Stoa Poikile, the “painted porch,” which is where the name comes from.

Its goal is a good, steady life (the Greeks called it eudaimonia, flourishing). The Stoics believed the only true good is virtue, meaning good character, and that most of our suffering comes not from events but from our judgments about them.

“People are disturbed not by things, but by the views they take of them.”Epictetus, Enchiridion 5
Stoicism is

Clear thinking about what matters

Focus on your own choices, act well, accept what you can't change, and care about other people.

Stoicism isn't

Being emotionless

Lowercase “stoic” means hiding feelings. The Stoics didn't ask you to suppress emotion. They asked you to examine the beliefs behind it.

02

Four Stoics to know

Zeno of Citiumc. 334–262 BCE

A merchant who, the story goes, turned to philosophy after a shipwreck. Founded the school. Almost none of his writing survives.

Senecac. 4 BCE–65 CE

Roman statesman, writer and adviser to Emperor Nero. His Letters give friendly, practical advice on time, grief and anger.

Epictetusc. 50–135 CE

Born enslaved, later a famous teacher. His student Arrian wrote down his lessons in the Discourses and the short Enchiridion (handbook).

Marcus Aurelius121–180 CE

Roman emperor. His private notebook, now called Meditations, was never meant for publication.

Where to start reading: the Enchiridion is short and blunt. Meditations is personal and calm. Seneca's Letters read like advice from a wise friend. All are old enough to be free online.

03

The dichotomy of control

This is the core Stoic idea. The Enchiridion opens with it:

“Some things are within our power, while others are not.”Epictetus, Enchiridion 1
Up to me

My judgments, choices and actions

What I believe, what I aim for, how I respond, how much effort I give, how I treat people.

Not up to me

Everything else

Other people's opinions and choices, the weather, the economy, my reputation, the past, and in the end even my body's health.

Put your energy into the first column. Accept the second calmly. That doesn't mean not caring about outcomes. It means aiming well and letting go of the result.

Example · a job interview
  1. Not up to you: whether they hire you.
  2. Up to you: preparing, showing up on time, answering honestly, following up.
  3. Measure success by how well you prepared and acted. That part you can always get right.

The Stoic archer image (from Cicero) says the same thing: the archer does everything to aim well, but once the arrow leaves the bow, a gust of wind is not his failure.

        04

        The four Stoic virtues

        For the Stoics, virtue is the only real good. Money, health and fame are “preferred indifferents”: nice to have, fine to pursue, but not what makes you good.

        VirtueWhat it meansEveryday version
        WisdomKnowing what is truly good, bad or neitherPausing to ask “Is this really a problem, or just my reaction?”
        CourageFacing fear, pain and hardship wellHaving the hard conversation you've been avoiding
        JusticeTreating others fairly and doing your part for the communityBeing honest at work, helping a neighbor, voting thoughtfully
        TemperanceSelf-control and moderationClosing the app, skipping the second dessert, not snapping back

        Stoics weren't loners. They saw every person as part of one human community and believed we have duties to others. Justice was central, not optional.

        05

        Four daily Stoic practices

        1. Morning preparation. Marcus Aurelius opens Book 2 of Meditations by reminding himself that today he'll meet people who are rude or ungrateful, and that he can still choose not to be harmed or angered. Spend one minute picturing your day and how you want to act.
        2. Negative visualization (premeditatio malorum). Briefly imagine losing something you have, like a job, your health or a loved one. The goal isn't gloom. It reduces shock if it happens and makes you grateful for what you have now.
        3. Voluntary discomfort. Seneca suggested setting aside a few days to live on plain food and rough clothes, asking “Is this what I feared?” Today that might be a cold shower or a day without your phone. It shows you can handle less.
        4. Evening review. Seneca describes ending each day by asking: What bad habit did I cure today? What fault did I resist? In what way am I better? Be honest, then let it go and sleep.

        Use negative visualization gently. Keep it brief and end on gratitude. If you have anxiety or are grieving, dwelling on losses may not help, so skip it and talk to someone you trust or a professional.

        FAQCommon questions

        Is Stoicism a religion?

        No. It's a philosophy. The ancient Stoics did believe the universe was ordered by reason (which they sometimes called God or Nature), but modern readers of every faith and none use the practical ideas.

        Does Stoicism mean not caring?

        No. Stoics cared a lot about acting well and about other people. They tried not to be ruled by things outside their control, like other people's opinions.

        Is Stoicism related to therapy?

        The founders of modern cognitive therapy, including Albert Ellis and Aaron Beck, cited Epictetus's idea that our judgments shape our feelings. But Stoicism is a philosophy of life, not a treatment. If you're struggling, talk to a professional.

        What about things partly in my control?

        Most real goals are mixed. Split them: your effort is up to you, the result isn't. Set goals around your actions (“study an hour a day”) instead of outcomes (“get an A”).

        Where do my journal entries go?

        Into your browser's local storage on this device only. They aren't uploaded, and they won't appear on another phone or computer. Use “Copy all entries” to keep a backup.
