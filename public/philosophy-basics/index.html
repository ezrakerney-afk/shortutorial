Philosophy Basics

01

What philosophy actually is

The word comes from Greek: philo (love) + sophia (wisdom). Philosophy is the careful study of basic questions that science and everyday life rest on but don't answer by themselves. Questions like: What makes an action right? What counts as knowledge? Do we have free will?

Philosophers don't settle these by experiment. They use arguments: reasons (called premises) that are supposed to support a conclusion. Then other philosophers look for weak spots.

Example · a classic argument
  1. All humans are mortal. (premise)
  2. Socrates is a human. (premise)
  3. So Socrates is mortal. (conclusion)
  • Valid means the conclusion follows: if the premises were true, the conclusion would have to be true.
  • Sound means valid and the premises really are true. An argument can be valid but unsound: “All cats can fly. Tom is a cat. So Tom can fly.”

Think like a philosopher. Define your words. Ask “how do you know?” Look for counterexamples. And state the other side's view so well they'd agree with your summary before you argue against it.

02

The five main branches

Philosophy has many fields, but most intro courses start with these five.

BranchBig questionExample puzzle
EthicsHow should we live? What is right and wrong?Is it ever OK to lie?
EpistemologyWhat is knowledge, and how do we get it?Can you be sure you're not dreaming?
MetaphysicsWhat is reality made of? What exists?Is your mind the same thing as your brain?
LogicWhat makes reasoning good or bad?Why is “everyone does it, so it's fine” a weak argument?
Political philosophyWhat makes government legitimate? What is justice?Why should anyone obey the law?

Other branches include aesthetics (beauty and art), philosophy of mind, philosophy of science and philosophy of religion.

03

Seven thinkers to know

A very short list from a very long history. Each one changed the questions people ask.

Confucius551–479 BCE · China

Taught that a good society grows from personal virtue, respect in relationships, and ritual. His ideas shaped East Asian ethics for centuries.

Socratesc. 470–399 BCE · Athens

Wrote nothing. Questioned people until their confident beliefs fell apart (the Socratic method). “The unexamined life is not worth living.”

Platoc. 428–348 BCE · Athens

Socrates' student. Wrote dialogues. Argued for a world of perfect Forms behind what we see. Told the Allegory of the Cave.

Aristotle384–322 BCE · Greece

Plato's student. Founded formal logic and wrote on almost everything. His ethics centers on character and living well (eudaimonia).

René Descartes1596–1650 · France

Doubted everything he could, and found one thing he couldn't: “I think, therefore I am.” A father of modern philosophy.

Immanuel Kant1724–1804 · Prussia

Argued morality comes from reason and duty. Treat people as ends in themselves, never merely as tools.

John Stuart Mill1806–1873 · England

Defended utilitarianism (do what brings the most happiness) and individual liberty in On Liberty.

This list is a starting point, not a ranking. Philosophy also has deep traditions in India, the Islamic world, Africa and elsewhere, plus many later voices. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy in the sources is a free, reliable place to keep going.

04

Three ways to think about right and wrong

Most debates in ethics use three big theories. Each grabs something true, and each has famous problems.

TheoryCore ideaLinked toCommon objection
Utilitarianism (a kind of consequentialism)The right act brings about the best overall results, like the most well-being.Bentham, MillCould justify harming a few people if it helps many.
Deontology (duty ethics)Some acts are right or wrong in themselves. Keep promises, don't lie, respect rights, whatever the results.KantRules can clash, and following one can lead to disaster.
Virtue ethicsAsk what a person of good character (honest, brave, fair, kind) would do, and become that person.Aristotle, ConfuciusCan be vague about exactly what to do in a hard case.
Example · should you tell a friend their business idea is bad?

Utilitarian: What will make things go best overall? Honest feedback now may save them money and pain later.

Deontologist: You have a duty not to deceive. Be truthful, though you don't have to be harsh.

Virtue ethicist: What would an honest and kind friend do? Find the tactful truth.

Notice they often agree on the answer. They differ on why, and that matters in the hard cases.

05

Famous thought experiments

A thought experiment is an imagined scenario that isolates one question so we can test our ideas. Here are three classics.

The trolley problem (ethics)

A runaway trolley will kill five people on the track. You can pull a lever to send it onto a side track, where it will kill one person. Do you pull it? Philosopher Philippa Foot raised a version of this in 1967, and Judith Jarvis Thomson added the footbridge twist: you could stop the trolley only by pushing a large person off a bridge into its path. The numbers are the same, yet most people say pulling the lever is OK and pushing is not. Why? That gap is the real puzzle.

The ship of Theseus (metaphysics)

The ancient writer Plutarch described the Athenians keeping the hero Theseus's ship, replacing rotten planks one by one. Once every plank has been replaced, is it the same ship? Thomas Hobbes later added: what if someone collected all the old planks and rebuilt them? Which one is the real ship? The puzzle is about identity over time, including yours: your body's cells are replaced too.

The brain in a vat (epistemology)

Imagine a scientist keeps a brain alive in a vat and feeds it signals that feel exactly like a normal life. How could you prove you aren't that brain? This is a modern version of Descartes' “evil demon” doubt, discussed by Hilary Putnam in 1981. It asks what, if anything, we can know for certain about the outside world.

The point isn't to “win.” A good thought experiment shows you which principles you actually hold, and where they conflict.

FAQCommon questions

Is philosophy just opinion?

No. Everyone can have a view, but philosophy asks you to give reasons, define terms and answer objections. Some arguments are much stronger than others, even when a question stays open.

What's the difference between philosophy and science?

Science answers questions by observation and experiment. Philosophy works on questions experiments can't settle alone, like what makes evidence good or what we owe each other. Science itself grew out of “natural philosophy.”

Which ethical theory is correct?

There is no consensus. Philosophers still defend all three theories, plus newer ones. Many people use them as different lenses on the same problem.

Where should a beginner start reading?

Plato's short dialogues about Socrates (like the Apology) are readable and free online. For overviews, the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy has free, expert-written entries on nearly every topic.

Is the trolley problem used in real life?

Versions of it come up in debates about medical triage, public policy and how self-driving cars should be programmed, though real cases are messier than the thought experiment.
