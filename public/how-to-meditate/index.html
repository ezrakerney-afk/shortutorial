How to Meditate

Meditation is simpler than it sounds: sit, pay attention to something like your breath, notice when your mind wanders, and come back. Learn the basics in a few minutes, then use the guided timer to practice.

01

What meditation is (and isn't)

Meditation is a set of practices for training attention. The kind most beginners start with is mindfulness: paying attention to the present moment, on purpose, without judging it. According to the National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH), many types of meditation share a few things: a quiet place, a comfortable posture, a focus of attention, and an open attitude toward distractions.

  • It isn't emptying your mind. Thoughts will keep coming. The practice is noticing them and returning to your focus.
  • It isn't about feeling blissed out. Some sessions feel calm, some feel restless. Both count.
  • It doesn't need special gear, beliefs or a cushion. A chair and a few minutes are enough.

Research summarized by NCCIH suggests meditation and mindfulness practices may help with things like stress, anxiety, depression symptoms, sleep and blood pressure, though results vary and many studies are small.

Safety: NCCIH says meditation is generally considered low risk for healthy people, but some people report unpleasant experiences, such as more anxiety. If you have a mental health condition or a history of trauma, talk with your health care provider, and don't use meditation to replace medical care or put off seeing a professional. If a practice makes you feel worse, stop, open your eyes, and try a shorter or different approach.

02

How to sit

Aim for a posture that's relaxed but alert. You want to be comfortable enough to stay still, but upright enough not to doze off.

  1. Seat: a chair with your feet flat on the floor is great. You can also sit on a cushion with legs crossed, kneel on a cushion, or lie down if sitting hurts.
  2. Back: sit tall but not stiff, as if a string were gently lifting the top of your head. Lean off the chair back if you can.
  3. Hands: rest them on your thighs or in your lap, whatever feels easy.
  4. Shoulders and jaw: let them drop and soften. Most of us hold tension here.
  5. Eyes: close them, or keep them half-open with a soft gaze at the floor a few feet ahead.

Physical limits are fine. If you have pain or an injury, adjust. Lying down, leaning against a wall, or even walking slowly all work.

03

Focus on the breath

The breath is a handy anchor because it's always with you. You don't need to control it. Just feel it.

  1. Take two or three slower breaths to settle, then let your breathing go back to normal.
  2. Pick one spot where you feel the breath most clearly: the air at your nostrils, your chest rising, or your belly expanding.
  3. Rest your attention there. Feel each in-breath and each out-breath.
  4. If it helps, silently count: “one” on the out-breath, up to ten, then start over.

04

When your mind wanders

It will. Probably within seconds. This isn't failure; noticing that you wandered and coming back is the actual exercise, like a single rep at the gym.

  1. Notice: “Oh, I'm planning dinner.”
  2. Label it gently, if you like, with one word: “planning,” “worrying,” “remembering,” “itch.”
  3. Return: bring your attention back to the breath. No scolding.
  4. Repeat as many times as needed. A hundred times in ten minutes is completely normal.
Example · a typical minute
  1. Breath in, breath out… “Did I reply to that email?”
  2. Notice: “thinking.” Back to the breath.
  3. Breath in… knee itches. Notice the itch without scratching for a moment. Back to the breath.
  4. Two reps of attention training. That's a good minute.
  • Sleepy? Sit up straighter, open your eyes a little, or meditate earlier in the day.
  • Restless? Try counting breaths, or do a shorter session.
  • Strong emotions? You can open your eyes, feel your feet on the floor, and come back later. You don't have to push through.
05

Body scan and a daily habit

A simple body scan

A body scan moves your attention slowly through the body, noticing sensations without trying to change them. It's a good option when the breath feels boring or you're winding down for bed.

  1. Lie down or sit comfortably. Take a few easy breaths.
  2. Bring attention to your toes. Notice warmth, coolness, tingling, pressure, or nothing at all. All are fine.
  3. Move slowly up: feet, ankles, calves, knees, thighs, hips.
  4. Then belly, chest, back, shoulders, arms, hands.
  5. Finish with neck, jaw, face and the top of the head.
  6. End by noticing the whole body breathing at once.

Spend about 20 to 60 seconds on each area. Set the timer above to 10 minutes with a 1-minute bell to know when to move on.

Make it a habit

  • Start tiny: 2 to 5 minutes a day beats 30 minutes once a week.
  • Anchor it to something you already do: after brushing your teeth, after your morning coffee, before you open your laptop.
  • Same place each day helps it become automatic.
  • Missed a day? Just start again. Don't try to make it up.
  • After a couple of weeks, add a few minutes if you want to.

Mini practice anywhere: take three slow breaths before you start the car, answer a hard email, or walk into a meeting. Feel your feet. That counts too.

FAQCommon questions

How long should a beginner meditate?

Start with 2 to 5 minutes. Consistency matters more than length. Many people settle into 10 to 20 minutes once it's a habit.

Am I doing it wrong if my mind keeps wandering?

No. Everyone's mind wanders. Each time you notice and come back, you're doing the practice.

Is it OK to meditate lying down?

Yes, especially for a body scan. You're just more likely to fall asleep. That's fine at bedtime.

What's the best time of day?

Whenever you'll actually do it. Morning is popular because it's before the day gets busy, but any regular time works.

Do I need an app?

No. A timer is enough. Guided recordings can help at first; many libraries and health systems offer free ones.

Can meditation replace therapy or medication?

No. It can be a helpful addition, but don't use it to replace care from a health professional. Talk with your provider about how it fits with your treatment.
