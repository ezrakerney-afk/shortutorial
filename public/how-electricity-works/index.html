How Electricity Works

What is actually moving in a wire? Why does a bulb get dimmer when you add another one in a row, but not when you add one side by side? Learn the four ideas behind every circuit, then build your own in the playground.

01

Charge and current

Everything is made of atoms, and atoms contain tiny particles with electric charge. Protons in the center are positive. Electrons around the outside are negative. Opposite charges attract and like charges repel. That push and pull is the force behind all electricity.

In metals like copper, some electrons are only loosely held, so they can drift from atom to atom. That's why metals are conductors. Rubber, plastic, glass and dry wood hold their electrons tightly, so they're insulators. A power cord is both: copper inside to carry the current, plastic outside to keep it away from you.

Current is charge flowing past a point. It's measured in amperes (amps, A). One amp means one coulomb of charge passes each second, and a coulomb is about 6.24 billion billion electrons.

current = charge ÷ time1 amp = 1 coulomb per second

Electrons are slow, the signal is fast. Each electron in a wire only drifts along at a fraction of a millimeter per second. The light comes on instantly because the wire is already full of electrons: push on one end and they all start moving at once, like marbles in a full tube.

Which way does current flow? Long before electrons were discovered, scientists picked "positive to negative" as the direction of current. Electrons actually move the other way. Circuit diagrams still use the old "conventional current" direction, and the math works the same either way.

For current to flow there has to be a closed loop, a circuit, from one side of the source back to the other. A switch simply opens a gap in the loop. Open the gap and the flow stops everywhere in that loop.

02

Voltage and resistance

The classic way to picture a circuit is water in pipes. It isn't perfect, but it gets the main ideas right:

Voltage · VPressureThe push that drives charge around the loop. Measured in volts.
Current · IFlow rateHow much charge moves per second. Measured in amps.
Resistance · RNarrow pipeHow hard it is for charge to get through. Measured in ohms (Ω).

Voltage is the difference in electrical "pressure" between two points. A battery uses a chemical reaction to keep one terminal at a higher voltage than the other. A AA battery gives 1.5 V, a car battery about 12 V, and US wall outlets about 120 V. Voltage is always measured between two points, which is why a bird can sit on a single power line safely: both feet are at the same voltage, so almost no current flows through it.

Resistance is how much a material fights the flow. Every part of a circuit has some. It goes up when a wire is:

  • Thinner, like a narrow pipe. That's why big appliances use thick cords.
  • Longer, because charge has farther to squeeze through.
  • Hotter, for most metals.
  • Made of a poorer conductor. Copper and silver are excellent. The tungsten in an old-style bulb is much worse, which is the point: resistance turns electrical energy into heat and light.

Resistance isn't bad. A toaster, a space heater and an incandescent bulb all work because of resistance. The trick is to put it where you want heat, and keep it low in the wires.

03

Ohm's law

In 1827 Georg Ohm showed that voltage, current and resistance are linked by one simple rule. More push means more flow. More resistance means less flow.

V = I × Rvolts = amps × ohms · so I = V ÷ R and R = V ÷ I

Know any two and you can find the third. A handy trick: cover the one you want in the triangle V over I × R. Cover I and you see V over R.

Example · a flashlight bulb
  1. A 3 V battery pack lights a bulb with 6 Ω of resistance.
  2. I = V ÷ R = 3 ÷ 6
  3. 0.5 amps flows through the bulb
Example · what's the resistance?
  1. A 120 V toaster draws 7.5 A.
  2. R = V ÷ I = 120 ÷ 7.5
  3. The heating element is 16 Ω (while it's hot)
Try it · Ohm's law solver

Real bulbs bend the rule a little. Ohm's law is exact for an ideal resistor. A bulb filament's resistance rises a lot as it heats up, so a cold bulb draws a big burst of current for a split second when switched on. The playground below treats each bulb as a fixed resistor to keep things clear.

04

Series and parallel circuits

Series

One path, in a row

Everything is on a single loop. The same current flows through each part, and the battery's voltage is shared between them. Resistances add up: R = R₁ + R₂ + R₃. Add a bulb and they all get dimmer. Break one and everything goes out.

Parallel

Side-by-side branches

Each part gets its own path. Every branch gets the full voltage, and the currents add up at the battery. Total resistance drops: 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃. Add a bulb and the others stay just as bright. Break one and the rest keep working.

Your home is wired in parallel. That's why every outlet gives the same 120 V, and turning off the lamp doesn't turn off the fridge. It also means each thing you plug in adds more current to the circuit. Plug in too much and the total current trips the breaker.

Old-style holiday light strings were wired in series, which is why one dead bulb could black out the whole string. Batteries in a flashlight are also in series: two 1.5 V cells in a row give 3 V.

05

AC vs DC

DC · direct current

Flows one way

The voltage stays steady and current flows in one direction. Batteries, solar panels and USB chargers give DC. Phones, laptops and LED circuits run on it.

AC · alternating current

Back and forth

The voltage swings positive and negative, so the current reverses direction over and over. In the US it does this 60 times a second (60 Hz). This is what comes out of your wall outlets.

Why use AC for the grid? Because transformers can easily raise or lower AC voltage. Power plants step it up to very high voltages for long-distance lines, which lets the same power travel with much less current. Less current means less energy wasted as heat in the wires. Transformers near your home step it back down. This advantage is why Westinghouse and Tesla's AC system won out over Edison's DC in the 1890s.

Most of your electronics actually need DC, so their chargers and power supplies convert AC to DC. That's what the "brick" on a laptop cord does.

120 V is an average. AC voltage is quoted as an effective (RMS) value that delivers the same heating as that much DC. The actual peaks of US household power reach about 170 V. US homes also get 240 V for big loads like dryers, ovens and many water heaters.

06

Power and safety

Power is how fast electrical energy is used, measured in watts (W). It's voltage times current:

P = V × Iwatts = volts × amps · also P = I² × R and P = V² ÷ R
Example · a space heater
  1. A heater draws 12.5 A from a 120 V outlet.
  2. P = 120 × 12.5
  3. 1,500 watts, one of the biggest loads you can put on a normal outlet

Your power company bills you for energy, which is power × time, in kilowatt-hours (kWh). A 1,500 W heater running for 2 hours uses 3 kWh. To see what that costs and which appliances are the biggest users, read Watts, kWh and your electricity bill.

P = I² × R explains why the grid uses high voltage, and why loose or undersized wires get hot: double the current through the same resistance and you get four times the heat.

How electricity hurts people

It's current through your body that does the damage, and it takes surprisingly little. Your skin's resistance drops a lot when it's wet, so the same voltage pushes much more current through you. OSHA gives these rough effects for 1 second of current from hand to foot:

CurrentEffect
1 mAFaint tingle
5 mASlight shock. Most people can still let go.
6–30 mAPainful shock, loss of muscle control. You may not be able to let go.
50–150 mAExtreme pain, breathing stops, severe muscle contractions. Death is possible.
1,000–4,300 mAThe heart's pumping stops. Death is likely.

A standard household breaker is 15 or 20 amps, that is 15,000 to 20,000 mA. OSHA notes that fuses and breakers protect wires and equipment from overheating, not people. That's the job of a GFCI (ground-fault circuit interrupter), the outlet with Test and Reset buttons. It shuts off power when it detects as little as about 5 mA leaking somewhere it shouldn't, in as little as 1/40 of a second.

  • Keep electrical devices away from water, and never touch them with wet hands.
  • Use GFCI outlets in bathrooms, kitchens, garages, basements and outdoors. Press Test and Reset once a month.
  • Don't overload outlets or daisy-chain power strips. Warm plugs, flickering lights or a burning smell mean trouble.
  • Replace cords that are frayed, cracked or pinched. Never run cords under rugs.
  • Turn off the breaker before any electrical repair, and confirm it's off with a voltage tester.
  • Stay at least 10 feet from overhead power lines, and assume any downed line is live. Call 911 and the utility.

If someone is being shocked, don't touch them. Shut off the power at the breaker or unplug the device first. If you can't, call 911 and wait. Once the power is off, call 911 and start CPR if they aren't breathing normally. See First aid basics.

FAQCommon questions

Is it the volts or the amps that kill?

The current (amps) through your body does the damage, but you need enough voltage to push that current through your skin. That's why a 12 V car battery is safe to touch with dry hands but 120 V from an outlet can kill. Static shocks can be thousands of volts but carry so little charge for so short a time that they're harmless.

Why does a bird on a power line not get shocked?

Both of its feet are on the same wire, at the same voltage. With no voltage difference across its body, almost no current flows through it. If it touched a second wire or a grounded pole at the same time, current would flow.

What does a circuit breaker actually do?

It's an automatic switch that opens when the current gets higher than the wires are rated for, usually 15 or 20 amps for household circuits. That stops the wires from overheating and starting a fire. It is not fast or sensitive enough to protect a person from shock. A GFCI does that.

Do electrons travel from the power plant to my house?

No. With AC, electrons mostly jiggle back and forth in place. What travels is energy, carried by the electric and magnetic fields along the wires at close to the speed of light.

What is a short circuit?

A path with almost no resistance, for example when two bare wires touch. By Ohm's law, very low resistance means a huge current, which can melt wires and start fires within seconds. Breakers and fuses are there to cut it off.

What's the difference between watts and watt-hours?

Watts measure how fast energy is used right now. Watt-hours (or kilowatt-hours) measure how much energy was used over time. A 10 W LED on for 100 hours uses 1,000 Wh, or 1 kWh.
