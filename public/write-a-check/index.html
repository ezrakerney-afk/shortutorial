How to Write a Check

Rent, a contractor, a gift, a government office that won't take cards: checks still come up. Here's how to fill one out correctly, plus how to void, endorse and keep track of them.

01

The parts of a check

Every personal check has the same six spaces for you to fill in, plus some printed numbers at the bottom.

SpaceWhereWhat goes there
DateTop rightToday's date
Pay to the order ofMiddle leftWho you're paying (the payee)
Amount boxRight, next to $The amount in numbers
Amount lineUnder the payee, ends in “Dollars”The amount in words
MemoBottom leftWhat it's for (optional)
SignatureBottom rightYour signature

Printed along the bottom are your bank's routing number (9 digits), your account number, and the check number, which also appears in the top right corner. You'll need the routing and account numbers to set up direct deposit.

02

Fill it out, step by step

Use a pen with blue or black ink, never pencil or erasable ink.

  1. Date. Write today's date, like 10/13/2026 or October 13, 2026.
  2. Payee. Write the full name of the person or company exactly, like Maria Lopez or City of Springfield Water Dept. Ask if you're unsure what name to use. Never write “Cash” unless you mean it: anyone holding it could cash it.
  3. Amount in numbers. In the box, write the amount close to the dollar sign so nothing can be added in front: 125.50. Always include the cents, even if it's .00.
  4. Amount in words. On the long line, write the dollars in words and the cents as a fraction over 100: One hundred twenty-five and 50/100. Start at the far left and draw a line through any leftover space up to the word “Dollars.”
  5. Memo. Optional, but helpful: October rent, Apt 4B or an account or invoice number.
  6. Sign. Sign the bottom right line the same way your name appears on the account. Sign last, after everything else is filled in.

Never sign a blank check. Anyone who gets it can fill in any amount. And don't leave gaps: empty space on the payee or amount line is where someone could add to it.

03

Writing the amount in words

The written amount is the legal amount of the check. If the words and numbers don't match, the bank goes by the words, or may refuse the check. So it's worth getting right.

  • Hyphenate 21 to 99: twenty-one, forty-five, ninety-nine.
  • Use “and” only once, before the cents: One thousand two hundred five and 00/100, not “one thousand two hundred and five.”
  • Cents go over 100: $7.05 is Seven and 05/100. No cents? Write and 00/100 or and no/100.
  • Under a dollar: $0.75 is Zero and 75/100, though most people just pay that in cash.

    04

    Voiding and endorsing

    Void a check

    An employer or a company setting up automatic payments may ask for a voided check so they can copy your routing and account numbers. Also void any check you mess up.

    1. Write VOID in large letters across the front in pen. Don't cover the numbers along the bottom if someone needs to read them.
    2. Write “VOID” in your check register next to that check number.
    3. Once it's no longer needed, shred it or cut it up. If that check number ever shows up as paid, call your bank right away.

    Endorse a check you received

    Before you can deposit or cash a check made out to you, you sign the back in the endorsement area near the top, on the left end. Don't write below the line that says not to.

    TypeWhat you writeWhen
    Restrictive“For deposit only” + signature (some banks also want your account number)Safest. It can only go into your account.
    Mobile depositSignature + “For mobile deposit only,” sometimes with your bank's nameDepositing with a phone app. Many banks require this exact wording, so check your app.
    BlankJust your signatureOnly at the teller window. If lost, anyone holding it could cash it.
    SpecialYour signature, then “Pay to the order of [name]” below itSigning it over to someone else. Their bank may not accept it, so ask first.

    05

    Keep your account balanced

    A check doesn't come out of your account until the other person deposits it, which could be days or weeks later. If you only look at your bank balance, you might spend money that's already promised. A check register fixes that: the small booklet in your checkbook, a notes app or a spreadsheet.

    1. Write it down right away: check number, date, payee and amount.
    2. Keep a running balance: subtract each check, debit card payment and fee; add each deposit.
    3. Reconcile monthly: when your statement arrives, tick off each item that cleared. Checks that haven't cleared yet are outstanding.
    4. Check the math: statement balance − outstanding checks + deposits not yet shown should equal your register balance. If not, look for a missed fee or a typo, and call the bank if you spot an error.

    FAQCommon questions

    What if I make a mistake on a check?

    Don't cross it out and keep going. Write VOID across it, note it in your register, and start a fresh check. A bank may refuse a check with changes on it.

    Can I postdate a check (write a future date)?

    You can, but don't count on it. In the U.S., a bank can still cash a postdated check before that date unless you've formally told your bank in advance. Only hand over a check when there's money to cover it.

    How long is a check good for?

    Personal checks are generally good for 6 months. After that, a bank isn't required to cash it, though it may. Some checks print a shorter limit, like “void after 90 days.”

    Where do I find my routing and account numbers?

    On the bottom of the check: the 9-digit routing number is first, then your account number, then the check number. They're also in your bank's app or website.

    What happens if the check bounces?

    If you don't have enough money in the account, the bank may return the check unpaid and charge you a fee, and the person you paid may charge one too. Keeping a register helps you avoid it.

    How do I write a check for cents only, like $0.50?

    Write 0.50 in the box and Zero and 50/100 on the line. Many people write “Only 50/100” or “Fifty cents” and cross out “Dollars”; ask the payee if they have a preference.
