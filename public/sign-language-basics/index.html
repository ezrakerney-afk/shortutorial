American Sign Language (ASL) Basics

ASL is a full language with its own grammar, history and culture. Start with the respectful basics: what ASL is, how to fingerspell the alphabet, count to ten, sign a few everyday words, and talk with Deaf people politely. Then drill your fingerspelling with the trainer.

01

What ASL is (and isn't)

American Sign Language is a complete, natural language used by Deaf communities in the United States and much of Canada. According to the National Institutes of Health (NIDCD), it has the same linguistic properties as spoken languages. It has its own rules for pronunciation, word formation and word order, and its grammar is different from English.

  • It's not English on the hands. ASL has its own sentence structure. "Signing English word for word" is a different thing.
  • It's not universal. Different countries have different sign languages. British Sign Language (BSL) is very different from ASL, and even uses a two-handed alphabet.
  • Your face is part of the grammar. Facial expressions and body movement carry meaning. For example, for a yes/no question, signers raise their eyebrows, widen their eyes and lean forward slightly.
  • It has history. No one person invented ASL. It grew more than 200 years ago from local sign languages in America mixing with French Sign Language. Today they are separate languages.
  • It has accents. Like any language, ASL has regional variations, slang, and signs that differ by age and community.

Deaf culture: "Deaf" versus "deaf"

The National Association of the Deaf (NAD) explains that lowercase deaf describes the audiological condition of not hearing, while capital-D Deaf refers to a group of people who share a language (ASL) and a culture. Many Deaf people see themselves as a cultural and language community, not as people with something missing.

A note on learning from a page. Signs move, and movement, location and facial expression all matter. Use this page for the basics and a vocabulary of handshapes, then watch the signs in motion on the video sites linked at the bottom. Best of all, learn from Deaf teachers.

02

Fingerspell the alphabet

Fingerspelling uses one handshape per letter to spell out English words. It's mostly used for names of people and places, brand names, and words that don't have a common sign. It's a small part of ASL, but it's the best place to start because you'll use it to introduce yourself.

How to fingerspell well

  1. Use your dominant hand, held up near your shoulder, palm generally facing the person you're talking to.
  2. Keep your hand in one place. Don't bounce or push it forward on every letter. Let the letters flow smoothly from one to the next.
  3. For double letters (like the two Ls in "Bell"), make a small slide to the side or a slight bounce, instead of making the letter twice from scratch.
  4. Steady rhythm beats speed. Even, clear letters are easier to read than fast, jerky ones.
  5. Read whole words, not single letters. Fluent signers read the overall shape and movement of a word, the way you read printed words without spelling them out.

03

Count from 1 to 10

ASL numbers are one-handed. Notice that 3 uses the thumb, index and middle fingers. It is not the "W" handshape. Numbers 6 to 9 are made by touching the thumb to one fingertip, while the other fingers stay up.

#Handshape
1Index finger up
2Index and middle fingers up and apart
3Thumb, index and middle fingers up
4Four fingers up and apart, thumb tucked into the palm
5All five fingers spread
6Thumb touches the pinky tip, other three fingers up
7Thumb touches the ring finger tip
8Thumb touches the middle finger tip
9Thumb touches the index tip (looks like the letter F)
10Thumbs-up from a fist (the A handshape), with a small twist or shake

Which way does the palm face? ASL University (Lifeprint) notes that when counting 1 to 5 on their own, native Deaf signers typically face the palm toward themselves. For a series of digits, like a phone number, and for multi-digit numbers, the palm faces out.

04

A few everyday signs

These signs are among the first that classes teach. In ASL writing, signs are often written in CAPITAL letters as a "gloss." The descriptions below are for a right-handed signer. Watch each one on a video dictionary before using it, because movement is hard to capture in words.

HELLO

Flat hand, fingers together, starts at the side of your forehead and moves out and away, like a small salute.

THANK YOU

Flat hand, fingertips near your chin or lips, moves forward and slightly down toward the person.

PLEASE

Flat hand on your chest, palm in, moves in a circle.

SORRY

A closed fist (A or S handshape) circles on your chest.

YES

A fist (S handshape) bobs up and down from the wrist, like a nodding head.

NO

Index and middle fingers close down onto the thumb, like a mouth snapping shut.

NAME

Both hands in the H shape. The fingers of your dominant hand tap twice across the fingers of the other.

HELP

A thumbs-up fist rests on your other flat palm, and both hands lift up together.

MORE

Both hands in a flattened O shape (fingertips touching thumb). Tap the fingertips of both hands together.

I LOVE YOU

One hand: thumb, index and pinky up, middle and ring fingers down. It combines I, L and Y.

Example · introducing yourself
  1. Sign HELLO.
  2. Sign MY NAME (flat hand on your chest for MY, then NAME).
  3. Fingerspell your name, smoothly, with your hand in one spot.
  4. Ask the other person for theirs with a questioning face: YOUR NAME WHAT? with eyebrows down.

Name signs are special signs for people's names. They're given to you by members of the Deaf community, so don't make one up for yourself. Until then, fingerspell your name.

05

Etiquette with Deaf people

  • Getting attention: wave your hand in their line of sight, gently tap a shoulder or arm, or flick the lights in a room. Don't grab or startle someone from behind.
  • Keep eye contact. Looking away while someone signs is like walking out mid-sentence. Watch the face, not just the hands.
  • Talk to the person, not the interpreter. Say "How are you?", not "Ask her how she is." Look at the Deaf person while you talk.
  • Don't walk between two signers if you can avoid it. If you must, go through quickly without stopping, and there's no need for a long apology.
  • Don't over-exaggerate your mouth or shout. Speak naturally if they lip-read, face them, and keep your mouth uncovered. Not all Deaf people lip-read, and lip-reading only catches part of what's said.
  • Writing is fine. A notepad or a phone's notes app is a perfectly good way to communicate. Use whatever works.
  • Use the words people prefer. Many in the community prefer "Deaf" or "hard of hearing" to "hearing impaired." Never use "deaf and dumb" or "deaf-mute."
  • Try your signs. Most Deaf people appreciate a hearing person who is genuinely learning. If you don't understand, it's fine to ask them to repeat or to sign more slowly.

Some knowledge isn't fluency. For medical, legal, school or work situations, the Americans with Disabilities Act requires many businesses, hospitals and government services to provide effective communication, which often means a qualified interpreter. Don't offer to interpret just because you've learned the basics.

FAQCommon questions

Is ASL hard to learn?

Like any language, it takes time. Fingerspelling and basic vocabulary come quickly. Grammar, facial expressions and reading other people's signing take regular practice, ideally with Deaf signers.

Should I learn ASL or "baby sign"?

"Baby sign" programs borrow a few ASL signs. If you want to talk with Deaf people, learn ASL itself. Many parents use real ASL signs with babies too.

Which hand should I use?

Your dominant hand does most of the work and all the fingerspelling. Left-handed signers simply mirror everything. Just stay consistent and don't switch hands mid-conversation.

Why can't I read other people's fingerspelling?

Everyone finds receptive fingerspelling harder at first. Fluent signers spell quickly and read word shapes, not letters. Watch fingerspelling videos often, and look for the first letter, the last letter, and the length.

Is ASL used outside the US?

ASL is used in the United States and much of Canada, and has influenced sign languages in other countries. Most countries have their own sign language, so ASL won't automatically work everywhere.
