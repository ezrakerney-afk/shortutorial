Knife Skills for Beginners

Cut faster, more evenly, and with all ten fingers intact. Learn how to hold a chef's knife, the classic cuts and their sizes, how to dice an onion, and how to keep your knife sharp. Then test your eye with the true-size cut trainer.

01

Choose your knife

You don't need a big block of knives. Almost everything in a home kitchen can be done with three:

KnifeTypical sizeUse it for
Chef's knife8 inchesNearly everything: chopping, slicing, dicing, mincing. If you buy one good knife, buy this.
Paring knife3 to 4 inchesSmall in-hand jobs: peeling, hulling strawberries, trimming.
Serrated (bread) knife8 to 10 inchesCrusty bread, tomatoes, anything with a tough skin and soft inside.

Parts of a chef's knife

A sharp knife is a safer knife. A dull blade needs more force and is more likely to slip off food and into your hand. Lesson 5 shows how to keep it sharp.

02

Hold it right: pinch grip and claw grip

Your two hands have different jobs. The knife hand controls the blade. The guiding hand holds the food and protects itself.

Knife hand

The pinch grip

Pinch the blade itself, just in front of the handle, between your thumb and the side of your bent index finger. Wrap your other three fingers around the handle. It feels odd at first, but it gives far more control than holding only the handle with a finger on the spine.

Guiding hand

The claw grip

Curl your fingertips under, like a cat's paw, and tuck your thumb behind them. Rest the flat side of the blade against your knuckles. The knuckles guide the knife and the fingertips stay out of its path. Walk the claw back as you cut.

Set up before you cut

  1. Steady the board. Put a damp paper towel or thin towel under it so it can't slide.
  2. Make a flat side. Round foods roll. Cut a thin slice off one side of an onion, potato or carrot and set it on that flat side.
  3. Stand square to the counter with the board at about hip height, and give yourself room.

Two basic motions

  • Rocking cut: keep the tip on the board and rock the heel up and down while moving through the food. Best for mincing herbs and garlic.
  • Push cut (forward slice): move the blade forward and down through the food in one smooth stroke, then lift. Best for slicing and dicing vegetables. Let the sharp edge do the work; don't chop straight down hard.

Never flatten your guiding hand on top of the food with fingers out, and never cut toward your body or toward a hand holding the food.

03

The basic cuts and their sizes

Recipes use these words to tell you how big to cut. Even pieces cook evenly, which is the real point. Sizes are the standard culinary-school dimensions.

CutSizeShape
Large dice¾ × ¾ × ¾ inCubes, for stews and roasting
Medium dice½ × ½ × ½ inCubes, for soups and sautés
Small dice¼ × ¼ × ¼ inCubes, for salsas and sauces
Brunoise⅛ × ⅛ × ⅛ inTiny cubes, cut from julienne
Batonnet¼ × ¼ × 2 inSticks, like thin fries
Julienne⅛ × ⅛ × 2 inMatchsticks (fine julienne is 1/16 in)
Paysanne½ × ½ × ⅛ inThin flat squares
MinceNo set size, very fineIrregular bits, for garlic, shallots, herbs
ChiffonadeThin ribbonsFor basil, mint, leafy greens

How to make them

  1. Slice: square off the food, then cut even slices (planks) the thickness you want for the final cut.
  2. Sticks: stack a few planks and cut them lengthwise into sticks of the same thickness. That's batonnet (¼ in) or julienne (⅛ in).
  3. Dice: line up the sticks and cut across them at the same spacing. Batonnet becomes small dice; julienne becomes brunoise.
  4. Mince: chop roughly, then lay your guiding hand flat on the spine of the knife (fingers up and away from the edge) and rock the blade back and forth over the pile, scraping it back together now and then.
  5. Chiffonade: stack the leaves, roll them up tightly like a cigar, and slice across the roll thinly. Fluff into ribbons.

Scrape with the spine, not the edge. Flip the knife over to push food across the board. Scraping with the edge dulls it fast.

04

How to dice an onion

The onion is the best practice food there is. The trick is to leave the root end on: it holds all the layers together while you cut.

  1. Trim the top. Cut off the stem (pointy) end. Leave the hairy root end alone, just shave off the whiskers.
  2. Halve through the root. Stand the onion on its flat top and cut straight down through the root, making two halves.
  3. Peel. Pull off the papery skin and the first tough layer if it's dry.
  4. Cut toward the root, not through it. Lay a half flat side down, root away from you. Make vertical cuts from near the root to the front, spaced the width you want (¼ in for small dice). Stop about ¼ inch before the root so the slices stay attached.
  5. Optional horizontal cuts. For a finer dice, hold the top flat with your palm and make one or two cuts parallel to the board, again stopping short of the root.
  6. Cut across. Turn to the claw grip and slice across the onion at the same spacing. Diced pieces fall away. Stop when you reach the root and set it aside for stock or the compost.
Example · medium dice for soup
  1. Space the vertical cuts about ½ inch apart
  2. One horizontal cut through the middle of a medium onion
  3. Cross cuts ½ inch apart
  4. About 1 cup of even ½-inch pieces from a medium onion

Fewer tears: a sharp knife crushes fewer cells, so it releases less of the irritating gas. Chilling the onion for a while before cutting helps some people too.

05

Honing vs sharpening

They sound the same but do different jobs.

Honing

Straightens the edge

A thin edge bends out of line with use. A honing rod (often called a "steel") pushes it back into line. It removes little or no metal. Do it often, every use or every few uses.

Sharpening

Grinds a new edge

A whetstone, an electric or pull-through sharpener, or a professional service grinds away metal to form a fresh edge. Do it when honing no longer brings the knife back, often a few times a year for a home cook.

How to hone safely

  1. Stand the rod straight up with its tip on a folded towel on the counter. Hold the handle firmly.
  2. Put the heel of the knife against the top of the rod at a small angle, roughly matching the edge (many Western knives are about 15 to 20 degrees per side; check your maker's advice).
  3. Draw the knife down and toward you so the whole edge, heel to tip, sweeps along the rod, ending with the tip near the bottom. Keep the edge pointing down at the towel, away from your body.
  4. Switch to the other side of the rod. Do about 5 to 10 light strokes per side, alternating. Light pressure is enough.

Is it sharp? Quick tests

  • Paper test: hold a sheet of printer paper by one corner and slice down through it. A sharp knife glides; a dull one snags or tears.
  • Tomato test: a sharp knife cuts into a tomato skin with almost no pressure.

Make the edge last

  • Hand wash and dry right away. Dishwashers knock knives around and dull them.
  • Cut on wood or plastic. Glass, stone, marble and ceramic plates wreck an edge.
  • Store it covered: a knife block, magnetic strip, or blade guard. Not loose in a drawer.
06

Knife and cutting board safety

These rules come from OSHA's restaurant safety guide for young workers, and they apply just as much at home.

Cutting board food safety

The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics' home food safety advice:

  • Use two boards: one only for raw meat, poultry and seafood, and one for ready-to-eat foods like bread and vegetables. Different colors make it easy.
  • Wash after every use in hot, soapy water, rinse, and air dry or pat dry with clean paper towels. Plastic, glass and solid acrylic boards can go in the dishwasher.
  • Sanitize after raw meat: mix a fresh solution of 1 tablespoon of unscented liquid chlorine bleach per gallon of water, flood the board, let it stand several minutes, then rinse and dry.
  • Replace worn boards with deep grooves, cracks or heavy knife scars. Bacteria hide in the grooves where washing can't reach.

If you cut yourself: press on it with a clean cloth or bandage and keep it raised. Minor cuts usually stop bleeding on their own. Get medical care if it's deep or gaping, won't stop bleeding with pressure, or later looks infected (spreading redness, more pain, warmth, swelling or drainage). Mayo Clinic notes you may need a tetanus shot if the wound is deep or dirty and it's been more than five years since your last one.

FAQCommon questions

What size chef's knife should a beginner get?

An 8-inch chef's knife is the most common choice. It's long enough for a melon and small enough to control. If your hands are small or your kitchen is tight, a 6-inch is fine. Hold one before you buy if you can: it should feel balanced and comfortable.

Wood or plastic cutting board?

Both are fine and both are kind to your knife. Plastic can go in the dishwasher. Whatever you choose, keep raw meat on its own board and replace boards that get deep grooves.

How often should I sharpen my knife?

Hone often, every use or every few uses. Sharpen when honing stops helping, which for most home cooks is a few times a year. If the paper test snags after honing, it's time.

Does a honing steel sharpen a knife?

Not really. It straightens a bent edge so it cuts like it's sharp again. Once the edge is worn down, only sharpening (removing metal) brings it back.

Can knives go in the dishwasher?

It's best not to. The heat, detergent and banging around dull the edge and can damage handles. Wash by hand and dry right away.

How do I get faster?

Go slowly and evenly first. Speed comes from practice with good technique, not from rushing. Dicing an onion a few times a week will make a big difference within a month.
